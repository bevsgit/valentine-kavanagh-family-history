Dublin & Cumbernauld · 1842 – 1958

The Valentines and the Kavanaghs

My family on the north side of Dublin. Four generations of bookbinders, coachmen and cloakmakers, the marriage in 1900 that joined the two sides, and the crossing to Scotland that ended the trade.

My great-great-grandparents married on 3 August 1863, in the Pro-Cathedral on Marlborough Street. William Valentine was a bookbinder of about twenty-one. Martha Mackey was a bookfolder of seventeen, and still legally a minor. He was a railway clerk's son and she was a coach maker's daughter, and both of them worked on books for the rest of their lives.

Ten years later, almost to the day, James Kavanagh married Anne Walsh in the same church. He was a widowed railway guard of twenty-six. She was sixteen. Their fathers, a coachman and a carpenter, lived a few doors apart on Jervis Street - which is almost certainly how they met.

Between them those two marriages produced fifteen children. In August 1900 the seventh Valentine child married the second Kavanagh child. That couple, Thomas and Anne, are my great-grandparents. They had thirteen children of their own - eleven in Dublin, and then, we think in 1916 or 1917, they crossed to a village in Scotland and had two more. My grandfather Walter was the seventh of the thirteen, and about six when they went.

I put this together from parish registers, civil records and three census returns. What follows is what the documents actually say, and I have tried to be clear about where they stop.

What the records show

Nearly everyone worked on books

William bound them, Martha folded them, and so did both their daughters. Louis set type and later moved onto a linotype machine. Thomas finished bindings and made foreman. The death certificates are the thing that stays with me. Four of them don't give the person a trade of their own, just a bookbinder's wife, a bookbinder's child, a bookbinder's son. Then Thomas gets to Scotland and by 1921 he's a railway porter.

They moved between churches more than once

William was Church of Ireland and Martha was Roman Catholic. The first two children were baptised Catholic at the Pro-Cathedral. From 1871 every child was baptised at St George's, Church of Ireland. By the 1901 census Elizabeth is down as Wesleyan and her sister Martha as Roman Catholic, in the same house. Thomas was baptised Church of Ireland in 1881 and married in the Pro-Cathedral as a Catholic in 1900. I can't explain any of it from the records, but it clearly bent to circumstance.

They buried a lot of children

Four of William and Martha's eleven died before they grew up. William Thomas of meningitis at eight. Ruth Florence of diphtheria at two. Walter William of typhus at thirteen. Mary probably of tuberculosis at nineteen, though I am not certain that record is hers. The Kavanaghs lost their first child, Patrick, at one month - the certificate says severe malnutrition. Thomas and Anne lost one too. The 1911 census records seven children born and six living, and the one missing is James.

And then they gave the trade up

On 19 June 1921 the census finds them at the Wynd in Cumbernauld Village. Thomas, Annie and twelve of their thirteen children - fourteen people in two rooms. Ten years earlier Thomas was a foreman bookbinder finisher. Here he is a railway porter for the North British Railway Company. William and Thomas John are in the local brick and fireclay works and Martha is in a warehouse. Whatever took them across, it wasn't the book trade.

Why I think they went

This part is my reading rather than the record. The value of what Thomas knew how to do had been falling away for his whole working life. Machines took the folding, the sewing, the casing-in and the blocking, and hand finishing shrank to a niche just as he reached the top of it. He was foreman of a trade that was going away, and there was no point carrying it to Scotland where the same machines were doing the same work. That is the push. The pull is that some of Anne's family had already made the move, and the understanding in the family is that they talked Thomas and Anne into following, on better prospects and better pay. Why he then went onto the railway I can't explain.

They moved constantly

More than twenty addresses turn up across these records and nearly all of them sit within a mile of the Pro-Cathedral. Thomas and Anne managed six Dublin addresses in sixteen years. The one settled stretch is Portland Place, where William and Martha stayed seventeen years and raised eight children. Several of these streets no longer exist - I have marked which on the map.

The bookbinder's trade

What William, Thomas and Louis actually did all day, and why the work changed under them.

A bookbinder took printed sheets from the printer and turned them into books. Folding and collating the gatherings, sewing them on a frame, attaching boards, covering in cloth or leather, then finishing: lettering, gilding, tooling, trimming, polishing. William's title, bookbinder, covered the whole craft. Thomas's title, book finisher, was the skilled decorative end of it.

Between 1880 and 1920 that craft split in two. Folding machines, sewing machines, casing-in machines, mechanical blocking presses and guillotines all arrived in the commercial binderies. Much of the work was deskilled. Women and children came into the trade in large numbers as factory hands, and by 1900 women outnumbered men in many binderies. Fine hand binding survived, but as a shrinking niche.

That is the arc my family lived through. Martha and both her bookfolder daughters were part of the female influx. Louis went from hand compositing to running a linotype machine. Thomas held on to the skilled end, and made foreman by 1911, just as that end was contracting. One Dublin firm of the period, Galwey & Co. of 22 Eustace Street, was big enough to take on institutional work of the first rank. My notes credit it with rebinding the Book of Kells in 1895. I have not been able to corroborate that against the standard accounts, which name 1826, an 1874 London rebinding and Roger Powell's in 1953. The firm and the premises are well documented. The 1895 job would need Trinity's own binding records to settle.
