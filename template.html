Dublin & Cumbernauld · 1842 – 1958

The Valentines and the Kavanaghs

Four generations of bookbinders, coachmen and cloakmakers on the north side of Dublin — the marriage, in August 1900, that brought the two families together, and the crossing to Scotland that ended the trade.

On 3 August 1863, in St Mary's Pro-Cathedral on Marlborough Street, a twenty-one-year-old bookbinder named William Valentine married a seventeen-year-old bookfolder named Martha Mackey. She was still legally a minor. He was the son of a railway clerk; she was the daughter of a coach maker. Both of them worked in the book trade, and both of them would stay in it for the rest of their lives.

Ten years later, almost to the day, a widowed railway guard called James Kavanagh married sixteen-year-old Anne Walsh in the same church. Their fathers — a coachman and a carpenter — lived a few doors apart on Jervis Street, which is almost certainly how they met.

Those two marriages produced fifteen children between them. In August 1900, the seventh Valentine child married the second Kavanagh child, and the families became one. They had thirteen children of their own — eleven in Dublin, and then, some time between the Easter Rising and the War of Independence, they crossed to a village in Scotland and had two more.

What the records show

A family of the book trade

William was a bookbinder; Martha a bookfolder; their daughters Elizabeth and Martha both bookfolders; their son Louis a compositor and later a linotype operator; their son Thomas a book finisher who rose to foreman. Four of the death certificates in this tree describe the deceased simply as a bookbinder's wife, a bookbinder's child, a bookbinder's son. It was not just a job — it was the family's identity in the eyes of the state. And then, in one census line, it stops.

A family that changed church

William was Church of Ireland, Martha Roman Catholic. Their first two children were baptised Catholic at the Pro-Cathedral. From 1871 every child was baptised at St George's, Church of Ireland. By 1901 Elizabeth is recorded as Wesleyan, Martha as Roman Catholic, and Thomas — baptised COI in 1881 — had married in the Pro-Cathedral as a Catholic. Denomination in this family bent to circumstance.

A family that buried its children

Of William and Martha's eleven children, four died before adulthood: William Thomas of meningitis at eight, Ruth Florence of diphtheria at two, Walter William of typhus at thirteen, and Mary — probably — of tuberculosis at nineteen. The Kavanaghs lost their firstborn, Patrick, at one month, of severe malnutrition. Thomas and Anne lost a son too: the 1911 census records seven children born and six living.

A family that gave up its trade

On 19 June 1921 the census finds them at the Wynd in Cumbernauld Village, Dunbartonshire: Thomas, Annie and twelve of their thirteen children — fourteen people in two rooms. Thomas, a foreman bookbinder finisher ten years earlier, is now a railway porter for the North British Railway Company. William and Thomas John are in the local brick and fireclay works; Martha is in a warehouse. Whatever took them across, it was not the book trade.

A family always on the move

More than twenty different addresses appear across these records, nearly all of them within a mile of the Pro-Cathedral. Thomas and Anne alone lived at six Dublin addresses in sixteen years. The one period of stability is Portland Place, where William and Martha stayed for seventeen years and raised eight children. Then the family left Ireland altogether.

The bookbinder's trade

Context for what William, Thomas and Louis actually did all day — and why the work changed under them.

A bookbinder took printed sheets from the printer and turned them into books: folding and collating the gatherings, sewing them on a frame, attaching boards, covering in cloth or leather, then finishing — lettering, gilding, tooling, trimming and polishing. William's title, bookbinder, covered the whole craft. Thomas's title, book finisher, was the skilled decorative end of it.

Between 1880 and 1920 that craft split in two. Folding machines, sewing machines, casing-in machines, mechanical blocking presses and guillotines arrived in the commercial binderies. Much of the work was deskilled, and women and children entered the trade in large numbers as factory hands — by 1900 women outnumbered men in many trade binderies. Fine hand binding survived as a prestigious but shrinking niche.

That is the arc this family lived through. Martha and both her bookfolder daughters were part of the female influx. Louis moved from hand compositing to operating a linotype machine. And Thomas — a foreman bookbinder finisher by 1911 — held on to the skilled end just as it was contracting. One Dublin firm of the period, Galwey & Co. of 22 Eustace Street, was substantial enough to take on institutional work of the first rank — your notes credit it with a rebinding of the Book of Kells in 1895, though that particular attribution is not one I have been able to corroborate against the standard published accounts of the manuscript's binding history, which name 1826, an 1874 London rebinding, and Roger Powell's in 1953. The firm and its premises are well documented; the 1895 job would need Trinity's own binding records to settle.
